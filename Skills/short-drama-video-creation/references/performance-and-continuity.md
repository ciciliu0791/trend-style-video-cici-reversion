# Performance and narrative continuity

## Make emotion visible

For a performance beat, describe `trigger → initial emotion → visible behavioral change → ending state`. Connect emotion to the main action or its result; an adjective alone is not a playable instruction.

Choose a small number of readable cues appropriate to the character, framing and visual style: gaze, expression, posture, distance, gesture or action rhythm. Do not demand a full-body signal in a face close-up. An expression reference may control performance while the identity reference continues to control appearance; name those roles explicitly.

A reaction shot is valid when it reveals a consequence, changes a relationship or lands a joke. It need not contain travel or a large prop action. Avoid sequences of interchangeable, unmotivated reactions. Give the audience time to perceive the change, with hold length fitted to the cut rather than a universal duration.

Check prompt conflicts before submission: a fixed neutral face versus visible relief, a closed-mouth constraint versus an open smile, or a fixed gaze versus looking toward a partner. Constraints must fit the shot and character; do not import animal anatomy or no-lip-sync rules into unrelated casts.

A compact prompt scaffold:

> In [visible location and opening state], [character] responds to [trigger] by [main action or purposeful reaction]. Their [initial emotion] changes through [visible cues]. Finish in [pose, gaze, prop state], with [appropriate hold or continued motion]. [Reference roles and shot-specific invariants].

## Carry state through cuts

For connected shots, preserve or deliberately change location, screen positions, eyelines, partner presence, prop state, unfinished action and emotional state. An interface insert changes what the audience sees; it does not by itself move the characters elsewhere. A shared frame should establish attention or interaction, not merely place two unrelated performances side by side.

First ask whether the audience needs to see the intervening action. Choose a direct cut, reverse shot, insert or action match when that communicates the relationship. Use a generated bridge when the intervening movement or state change must actually be shown. Detailed bridge mechanics remain in [scene timing and transitions](scene-timing-and-transitions.md).

For prop interactions, establish support, orientation and contact points before adding motion. Prefer staging that makes the intended action physically readable. Simplifying a grip into a tap is appropriate only when it preserves the brief; do not silently replace a required action.

## Review performance in context

Watch the trigger, response and landing with neighboring shots. Check whether the intended emotion is visible, identity is stable, gaze has a target and the response has enough reading time. Inspect movement peaks and mid-shot occlusions, including background figures and prop changes; clean first and last frames do not establish a clean action. Keep planned performance separate from observations of the returned clip.
